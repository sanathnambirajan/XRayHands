X-Ray Hands: looking inside CT scans with your bare hands

Cut, peel and probe real industrial X-ray CT scans in mixed reality, and pull the hidden defects out into a 3D chart.
Group 07Mithulram Gunasekaran (116787) · Sanath Nambirajan (119546)
ChallengeC2: Beyond Point-and-Click – Native 3D Interactions for Complex Data

1. Project goal

When engineers check an X-ray CT scan of a metal part for pores and cracks, they usually scroll through hundreds of 2D slices or turn a 3D rendering with mouse and menus. Where the defects actually sit in 3D has to be pieced together in their head.

X-Ray Hands puts the scanned part in front of the user in their real room (Meta Quest 3, passthrough). The hands are the only tool: a flat palm cuts the part and shows the real CT slice, two palms peel the metal away so only the pores stay, a finger probes the material with sound, and a pinch-and-pull sends every pore into a linked 3D scatter plot. There are no menus and no controllers. Our goal is that a user can find, compare and check defects in one place.

Where can 3D visualization be helpful?

  • A CT scan is already a 3D object; in 3D you see where pores cluster without rebuilding it from slices.
  • The cut plane follows the hand, so any angle is possible, not just the three fixed slice axes.
  • Pores and the chart sit in the same space, so it is easy to see which dot belongs to which pore.

Which 2D visualizations work better in 3D?

  • 2D slice viewer → a slice on the cut face of the real 3D part.
  • 2D scatterplot of defect features → 3D scatter plot (size × shape × height), linked to the part.
  • Threshold slider → peeling with two hands.

3. Dataset and format

We use two real, openly licensed CT scans. Both were processed with our own Python pipeline (crop, filter, find the pores or parts, compute features) and saved in a simple format that Unity loads.

A · 3D-printed metal sample (main dataset)B · Enigma I cipher machine rotors
SourceNIST, cobalt-chrome part made by laser powder bed fusion (doi:10.18434/M32162), incl. reference pore segmentationDeutsches Museum, scanned by Fraunhofer IIS/EZRT (doi:10.5281/zenodo.8232721)
Original900 × 1010 × 984 voxels, 2.45 µm, 16-bit TIFF549 slices of 784 × 551 px, 0.181 mm, 16-bit DICOM
In the app214³ voxels (central 1.57 mm cube)125 × 196 × 135 voxels
Content2,910 pores: 2,775 gas pores, 132 lack-of-fusion, 3 crack-like790 parts (contacts, pins, springs) grouped by density
LicenseNIST open licenseCC BY-SA 4.0

Format per dataset: volume_u8.raw (grey values) · labels_u16.raw (which pore/part each voxel belongs to) · features.csv (one row per pore: volume, diameter, sphericity, elongation, position, type) · meta.json (size, voxel spacing, license).

Check against the NIST reference: our pore detection finds 75 % of the reference pores (recall), and 90 % of what we find is a real pore (precision).

6. Implementation and libraries

  • Unity 6.3 LTS (URP), C#, with OpenXR, Meta OpenXR, XR Hands and AR Foundation for hand tracking and passthrough.
  • Own code: gesture recognizer, volume renderer (HLSL shader), interaction tools, 3D chart, sound probe.
  • Python 3.11 for the data pipeline: numpy, scipy, scikit-image, pydicom, tifffile, pandas.
